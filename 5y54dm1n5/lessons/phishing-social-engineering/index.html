Phishing & Social Engineering

How phishing attacks work, the red flags that give them away, and how to respond when one lands.

Social engineering: attacking the person, not the system

Most break-ins don't start with exploiting a technical flaw — they start with tricking a person into doing something that bypasses the technical defenses entirely: clicking a link, running an attachment, or just reading a password out over the phone to someone who sounds like IT. Phishing is the most common form: a fake email, text, or website impersonating something trustworthy to harvest credentials or deliver malware.

The red flags that give it away

A few signals show up in most phishing attempts: a sense of urgency ("your account will be suspended in 24 hours"), a mismatched sender address that doesn't quite match the real domain, a link whose actual destination (visible by hovering, not clicking) doesn't match its displayed text, and requests for something a legitimate sender would never actually ask for over email — a password, a gift card, a wire transfer. Any one of these alone isn't proof; several together is a strong signal to stop and verify through a separate channel before acting.

Variants worth knowing by name

Spear phishing targets a specific person with research-backed detail (using a real coworker's name, a real project) rather than a generic blast. Vishing is the same idea over a phone call instead of email. Smishing is phishing by text message. The delivery channel changes; the underlying trick — impersonate something trusted, create urgency, get the target to act before they think — stays the same.

Why this matters day to day

As IT support, you're often the first call when someone suspects they clicked something they shouldn't have — and the right response is the same every time: don't shame them for reporting it (that just trains people to hide the next one), have them change the affected password immediately, and check for signs of compromise on the account before closing it out. A reported phishing click handled well costs a few minutes; an unreported one can cost a lot more.
